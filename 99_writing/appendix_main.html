Appendix E. Supplementary Analyses to Analysis 3

Mapping the Field: Learnings from a Quarter-Century of Consumer-Technology Research

Abstract
Consumer technology research continually shifts as new technologies emerge, challenging cumulative science in a domain where the empirical object itself keeps changing. We build a quarter-century overview of how the field adapts and accumulates knowledge by screening 10,000 articles from seven leading marketing and consumer research journals and mapping out 16 technology clusters through semantic modeling. Specifically, we (1) map how academic attention moves across successive technology waves, (2) examine co-citation, historiographic coupling, and journal citation patterns to determine whether domains build on shared intellectual foundations, and (3) compare these trajectories with media attention to assess whether the field redirects its attention toward technologies that become salient in its external environment. We find distinct academic waves that are generally weakly aligned with media attention but remain substantially intellectually interconnected. Benchmarking Generative AI against these historical patterns, we find that it has been adopted across journals at unprecedented speed, yet rests on a comparatively narrow intellectual foundation and draws on earlier technology clusters less than those clusters already draw on it. We derive implications for researchers, and for journals, whose agenda-setting role makes them central to ensuring that attention to emerging technologies advances cumulative rather than merely novel knowledge.

Appendix A.

To fit the technology-clustering model reported in the main text, we swept the embedding model, the deduplication threshold, the number of clusters (\(k\)), and the number of UMAP dimensions used for clustering, evaluating each combination on silhouette, Davies-Bouldin, and Calinski-Harabasz scores, and, for the dimensionality sweep, trustworthiness against the original embedding space.

Embedding model

We compared three sentence-embedding models — sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2, BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5, and nomic-ai/nomic-embed-text-v1.5 — across every tested combination of \(k\) and deduplication threshold (Table 1). all-MiniLM-L6-v2 achieved the highest mean silhouette score overall (0.435), ahead of BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5 (0.415) and nomic-ai/nomic-embed-text-v1.5 (0.408), and was therefore selected as the embedding model for the final clustering solution.

Number of clusters (k) and deduplication threshold (d)

Holding the embedding model and deduplication threshold fixed, silhouette scores rose gradually across the swept range of \(k = 10\) to \(k = 20\), without a clear inflection point marking a single statistical optimum (Table 2): \(k = 16\) reached a silhouette of 0.436, the next value tested (\(k = 17\)) offered no improvement (0.433), and the score climbed gradually through the top of the swept range (\(k = 20\): 0.471). Because the statistical criteria did not identify a single unambiguous optimum, we prioritized interpretability: \(k = 16\) produced clusters that mapped onto substantively distinct, non-overlapping technology domains upon manual inspection, and we report it as the final solution while acknowledging that larger solutions were better by silhouette alone.

We also swept the deduplication threshold (\(d\)) used to collapse near-duplicate technology labels prior to clustering. Cluster fit statistics did not favor a stricter threshold: at the chosen model and \(k = 16\) (Table 3), \(d = 0.95\) reached the highest silhouette (0.441), \(d = 0.90\) reached 0.436, and \(d = 0.92\) performed worst of the three (0.431). More importantly, raising the threshold did not translate into more meaningful deduplication in practice: even at \(d = 0.95\), semantically identical near-duplicate labels such as “smartphone” and “smart phone” remained separate, unmerged technologies. Because a stricter threshold neither reliably improved cluster fit nor produced more meaningful merges, we retained \(d = 0.90\) as the deduplication threshold for the final clustering solution.

Dimensionality (UMAP components)

For the number of UMAP dimensions used in clustering, we evaluated trustworthiness — how well the reduced space preserves the original embedding space’s local neighborhood structure — alongside the cluster fit statistics, at the chosen model, threshold, and \(k = 16\) (Table 4). Trustworthiness rose sharply from 2 to 5 dimensions and then plateaued through 50 dimensions (0.92 at 5 dimensions, 0.924 at 10, 0.921 at 50), while silhouette and Davies-Bouldin scores at the lowest dimensionality tested (2 components) were inflated relative to this plateau — a well-documented tendency of clustering metrics to overstate separation in very low-dimensional projections (Aggarwal et al., 2001; Chari & Pachter, 2023; McInnes et al., 2020). We selected 10 dimensions as the point at which the reduced space had already captured the local structure of the original embeddings, without the artificial separation inflating fit statistics at the lowest dimensionalities tested.

Cluster Visualization and Temporal Analysis

Cluster assignments were merged back onto the paper-level dataset via the canonical technology label, restricted to consumer-facing, empirical mentions, and clusters were manually labelled based on their constituent technologies. Publication years were grouped into two-year intervals spanning 2000–2026, anchored to the most recent year with any remaining span absorbed into the earliest interval, and article counts per cluster were tabulated across intervals. Three temporal visualizations were produced from these tabulations: a line chart of raw article counts per cluster over time, a 100%-stacked-area chart of each cluster’s share of articles per interval (overlaid with the total article count per interval), and an absolute stacked-area chart of article counts per interval. A semantic overlay map, analogous to a VOSviewer network map, was also produced: each technology was plotted at its two-dimensional UMAP coordinates, sized by mention frequency and colored by cluster, alongside cluster centroids positioned at the frequency-weighted mean coordinates of their constituent technologies and sized by total cluster frequency.

Temporal Trajectories

Method

Journal Technology Mix

Method

  • Seven marketing journals: IJRM, JAMS, JCP, JCR, JM, JMR, MkSc.
  • Sample: empirical consumer-technology articles, 2000–2026, with a technology cluster assigned.
  • Unit: mention; an article naming several technologies contributes one mention per technology.
  • Counts cross-tabulated as journals (7) × technology clusters (16).

Deviation Test

  • Question: does a journal’s technology mix differ from the corpus as a whole?
  • Baseline b: overall technology cluster shares across the whole corpus, b_k = (Σ_j n_jk) / N.
  • Baseline includes the journal being tested, so all seven rows are read against one fixed reference point rather than seven shifting ones.
  • Validity: each mention falls in exactly one technology cluster, so a journal’s shares sum to 1 and the composition is proper; Dirichlet-multinomial applies.
  • Prior: Dirichlet, concentration α_k = K · b_k with K = 16 technology clusters, identical for every journal.
  • Prior mean = baseline, i.e. “this journal’s mix matches the corpus”.
  • Prior mass Σα_k = K, the same total weight as a flat Dirichlet(1, …, 1), redistributed proportionally rather than uniformly; equivalent to 16 pseudo-mentions of evidence for the null.
  • Effect: shrinks each journal toward the baseline, so small journals are pulled almost fully to the null and marks are harder to earn.
  • Posterior: Dirichlet-multinomial conjugacy, Dirichlet(α_k + n_jk) for journal j.
  • Sampling: gamma normalization, g_k ~ Gamma(α_k + n_jk, 1) then p_k = g_k / Σ g_k; 20,000 draws per journal, each a 16-vector summing to 1; single fixed seed.
  • Deviation per draw: d_k = p_k − b_k.
  • Interval: equal-tailed 95% credible interval, 2.5th and 97.5th percentile of the draws, read off the empirical quantiles.
  • Intervals marginal per cell, no multiplicity correction across the 7 × 16 cells.
  • Marks: ^1^ interval entirely above zero, ^2^ entirely below, blank spans zero.
  • Blank conflates no effect with too little data.
  • Marks concern share of a journal’s output, not counts; a small journal can be marked on modest absolute numbers.
  • Corpus is a census, so intervals generalize to the ongoing publishing process rather than to an unknown fixed quantity.

Results

  • Table 6 reports mentions per technology cluster and journal.
  • Rows are the 16 technology clusters, alphabetical with Other last; Total gives the cluster size across all seven journals.
  • Cell shares are row-wise, the share of the cluster’s mentions falling in that journal.
  • Marks are column-wise, on the journal’s own technology composition, so cell share and mark use different denominators.
  • ^1^ a cluster credibly over-represented in that journal relative to its corpus-wide share, ^2^ credibly under-represented, blank no credible deviation.
  • Figure 2 shows the counts of Table 6 as bar charts: top panel across all seven journals, lower panels one per journal.
  • The journal citation network based on the same counts is reported in Appendix C.

Appendix C.

Introduction

  • This appendix documents how the corpus of consumer-technology research relates to itself and to the wider body of literature it draws on.
  • The corpus is the deduplicated set of academic articles used throughout the paper, each assigned to one of the 16 technology clusters.
  • Three perspectives are reported, each answering a different question about the same corpus:
    • Internal connectedness — does the corpus build on itself? Which technology clusters cite which other technology clusters, measured on direct citation links between articles inside the corpus.
    • Shared knowledge foundation — which references does the corpus build on in common? Measured on co-citation, that is, references that corpus articles cite together, described both by the co-citation clusters themselves and by the Scopus classification of the cited sources.
    • Bibliographic coupling — a more fine-grained view on the same question: which technology clusters draw on overlapping reference lists, measured on the number of references that pairs of corpus articles share.
  • All three perspectives use the same technology cluster assignment, so the 16 clusters are directly comparable across sections.
  • A fourth section reports the same citation network by journal rather than by technology cluster: which journals cite which.

Internal Connectedness

Method

  • Direct citation links were extracted with histNetwork() from the bibliometrix package, restricted to the 743 corpus articles, so that only citations from one corpus article to another corpus article are counted.
  • The network runs on one record per article, so an article is a single node regardless of how many technologies it names.
  • Cluster membership is assigned afterwards and kept multiple: an article naming technologies from several clusters belongs to each of them, and a tie between two such articles counts once per cluster pair it spans.
  • Every citation link was labelled with the technology cluster of the citing article and the technology cluster of the cited article.
  • Links were aggregated to the cluster level in both directions, giving two directed 16 × 16 grids:
    • cites — how many citations the row cluster makes to the column cluster.
    • cited by — how many citations the row cluster receives from the column cluster.
  • The diagonal reports citations within the row cluster itself.
  • Each cell carries a raw count, the cross-citation share \(CC_{ij}\) of row cluster \(i\) with column cluster \(j\), and the ratio \(r_{ij}\).
  • In the cites grid, \(CC_{ij}\) divides the cell by all citations the row cluster makes within the corpus: how its outgoing attention is distributed.
  • In the cited-by grid, \(CC_{ij}\) divides the cell by all citations the row cluster receives within the corpus: where its incoming attention comes from.
  • Rows sum to 100% in both grids, so \(CC_{ij}\) describes a cluster’s profile rather than its volume, and clusters of different size stay comparable.
  • \(\overline{CC}_j = \frac{1}{15}\sum_{i \neq j} CC_{ij}\), the average share the other clusters record for that same column cluster. eq j} CC_{ij}$, the average share the other clusters record for that same column cluster.
  • The average is taken over the fifteen other rows, excluding the column cluster’s own row, so a cluster’s internal citation does not set its own reference point.
  • \(r_{ij} = CC_{ij} / \overline{CC}_j\), so 1 = the usual amount of attention for that counterpart, 2 = twice as much, 0.5 = half.
  • On the diagonal, \(r_{ii}\) states how much more of its attention a cluster keeps internally than the other clusters direct at it.
  • \(CC_{ij}\) is read along a row, not down a column; the two grids are not transposes of one another, because they carry different denominators.
  • Code: 30_academic_foundation/07_citation_network.R; output: cites_by_table.csv / .md and cited_by_table.csv / .md.

Results

  • Figure 3 shows the direct citation network of the corpus, with articles coloured by technology cluster and edges running from citing to cited article.
  • Table 7 and Table 8 quantify the same structure as counts, \(CC_{ij}\) and \(r_{ij}\).
  • Rows and columns are labelled with the technology cluster name.
  • Reading a row of Table 7 gives how one technology cluster spreads its citations, reading the same row of Table 8 gives where its citations come from.
  • Comparing the two tables cell by cell gives the direction of the relationship between two clusters.
  • The diagonal \(CC_{ii}\) states how much of a cluster’s citing, and of its being cited, stays inside the cluster.

Co-Mention Within the Same Article

  • A measure of connectedness that does not rely on citations, reported alongside the citation grids because it answers the same question from the other side.
  • Unit: article. For every pair of technology clusters, the number of corpus articles that name a technology from both.
  • An article mentioning several technologies of the same cluster counts once for that cluster, so the cell counts articles rather than mentions.
  • The matrix is symmetric and the diagonal is empty, because a cluster cannot co-occur with itself.
  • The final column sums the row and therefore counts co-occurrence links rather than articles: an article naming three clusters contributes two to each of those clusters.
  • The cross-mention rate is the share of a cluster’s own articles that also mention a technology from another cluster, counting each article once, and is therefore not affected by the size of the cluster.
  • Read against the citation grids above, a cluster can be strongly self-citing yet frequently co-mentioned, or the reverse, because the two measures track different behaviours.

Shared Knowledge Foundation

Method

  • Co-citation counts how often two references are cited together by the same corpus article, which identifies the bodies of work the corpus builds on in common, including references outside the corpus.
  • Before clustering, the most cited references were screened by hand and pure method papers, that is, references concerned solely with a method and not with its application to any topic, were removed.
  • The co-citation network was built with biblioNetwork(analysis = "co-citation", network = "references") on the deduplicated corpus and drawn with networkPlot() using a Fruchterman-Reingold layout, the 500 most cited references, a minimum edge weight of 3, Louvain community detection, a community repulsion of 0.8, and a fixed seed.
  • The same network is reported from two perspectives, so the nodes and edges are identical in both and only the colouring differs:
    • Louvain co-citation clusters — the communities the algorithm itself finds in the co-citation network, that is, groups of references that are habitually cited together.
    • Scopus classification of the cited sources — each cited reference matched to its source journal in the Scopus source list and labelled with the 4 Scopus top-level fields and the Scopus broad subject areas of that source.
  • For the second perspective, a reference was counted once per article and field, so an article citing several sources from the same field contributes once.
  • In the custom rendering, each reference node is additionally coloured by the technology cluster that dominates its citing articles, using a dominance threshold of 0.25; nodes below the threshold are labelled as having no dominant cluster.
  • Deviation test for the Louvain base clusters, same procedure as the journal test in Appendix B:
    • Unit: matched article-reference mention; one reference belongs to exactly one base cluster.
    • Counts: technology clusters (16) × base clusters; cell = mentions of that cluster in that base cluster.
    • Baseline b: overall base cluster shares across all technology clusters, column sums over the whole table.
    • Validity: categories mutually exclusive and exhaustive, row shares sum to 1, so the composition is proper and Dirichlet-multinomial applies.
    • Prior: Dirichlet, concentration α = b × number of base clusters, identical for every technology cluster.
    • Prior mean = baseline, i.e. “this cluster’s reference mix matches the corpus”.
    • Prior mass = number of base clusters, the same total weight as a flat Dirichlet(1, …, 1), redistributed proportionally; as uninformative as the flat prior, shrinking small clusters toward the baseline.
    • Posterior: Dirichlet-multinomial conjugacy, Dirichlet(α + row counts).
    • Sampling: gamma normalization, 20,000 draws per technology cluster, each a full vector summing to 1; fixed seed.
    • Deviation per draw: d = posterior share − baseline share.
    • Interval: equal-tailed 95% credible interval, 2.5th and 97.5th percentile of the draws.
    • Marks: ^1^ interval entirely above zero, ^2^ entirely below, blank spans zero.
    • Intervals marginal per cell, no multiplicity correction.
    • Not applied to the Scopus tables: an article can cite several fields, rows do not sum to 1, so the composition is not proper.
    • Mentions within one article are correlated, so the effective sample size is below the mention count and the intervals are narrower than the paper-level evidence warrants.
  • Code: 30_academic_foundation/06_cocitation_analysis.R and 30_academic_foundation/03_create_cluster_field_overview.R.

Bibliographic Coupling

Method

  • Bibliographic coupling counts how many references two corpus articles share, so it measures overlap in reference lists directly at the level of article pairs rather than at the level of reference communities.
  • It is reported here as a more fine-grained view on the Louvain co-citation clusters of the previous section: co-citation groups references that are cited together, whereas coupling states, for each pair of technology clusters, how much of their reference base is literally the same.
  • The coupling network was built with biblioNetwork(analysis = "coupling", network = "references") on the deduplicated corpus.
  • All unique article pairs were extracted from the coupling matrix and labelled with the technology cluster of both articles.
  • Pairs with no shared reference were retained, so that clusters of different size remain comparable.
  • The reported statistic is the mean coupling strength: the average number of shared references across all possible article pairs of the row and the column cluster.
  • The diagonal reports pairs of articles inside the same technology cluster.
  • Code: 30_academic_foundation/05_bibliographic_coupling.R; output: cluster_coupling_matrix.csv and cluster_coupling_matrix.md.

Results

  • Table 14 reports the mean coupling strength for all 16 × 16 cluster combinations.
  • Row labels carry the cluster number and the cluster name; column headers carry the cluster number only.
  • The matrix is symmetric, because coupling is undirected.
  • Higher values indicate that a randomly drawn article pair from the two clusters shares more references; a value of 0 indicates that no article pair between the two clusters shares any reference.
  • Comparing a diagonal cell to the remainder of its row gives the balance between reference overlap inside and outside the own technology cluster.

Journal-Level View

Method

  • Same within-corpus citation network as above, aggregated to journals instead of technology clusters.
  • Directed 7 × 7 grid; cells report cited by / cites.
  • Diagonal reports within-journal citations, where both numbers would describe the same links.
  • Deviation test on the same grid, same procedure as the technology mix test in Appendix B.
  • Run twice, once on the citations a journal makes and once on the citations it receives, because the two carry different denominators.
  • Diagonal excluded and each row renormalised over the six other journals, so the test asks where a journal directs its outward attention rather than how much it cites itself.
  • Baseline: the pooled distribution of off-diagonal citations across the seven journals, one fixed reference point for all rows.
  • Prior: Dirichlet, concentration α_k = 6 · b_k, prior mean = baseline, so small journals are pulled toward the null.
  • Posterior: 20,000 draws per journal, equal-tailed 95% credible interval on the deviation from the baseline.
  • Intervals marginal per cell, no multiplicity correction across the 42 off-diagonal cells of each grid.
  • Marks: ^1^ interval entirely above zero, ^2^ entirely below, blank spans zero.
  • The mark on the first number of a cell concerns the column journal’s citing profile, the mark on the second the row journal’s.

Appendix D.

Introduction

  • Two questions on what predicts how often a corpus paper is cited.
  • Early adoption: does entering a technology cluster early pay off?
  • Citation breadth: does citing within one’s own cluster or across clusters pay off?
  • Each estimated overall, across all 16 technology clusters, and separately per cluster.
  • Descriptive and observational; no causal identification.

Shared Setup

Method

  • Sample: corpus papers present in the within-corpus citation network, each with a technology cluster and a publication year.
  • Two outcomes, reported side by side:
    • Within Corpus — local citations, citations received from other corpus articles.
    • Total — global citations, citations received from anywhere.
  • Model family: negative-binomial GAM (mgcv::gam, family = nb()), appropriate for over-dispersed counts.
  • Publication age (age = 2026 − PY) entered as a smooth, so no decay shape is assumed.
  • Age is discrete; the basis dimension is capped below the number of distinct ages available.
  • Overall models additionally control for cluster citeability, the mean global citations of the paper’s cluster.
  • Per-cluster models omit that control, since it is constant within a cluster.
  • Coefficients are on the log scale of the expected citation count.
  • Code: 30_academic_foundation/07_citation_network.R.

Early Adoption

Method

  • Predictor: early adoption score = publication year − year of the earliest paper in the same technology cluster.
  • Lower score = earlier entry into the cluster; 0 = the cluster’s first paper.
  • Negative coefficient = earlier papers accumulate more citations.
  • Overall specification: citations ~ early adoption score + cluster citeability + s(age).
  • Per-cluster specification: citations ~ early adoption score, fitted separately for each of the 16 clusters.
  • Output: impact_early_adoption_per_cluster.csv / .md.

Citing Within and Across Technology Clusters

Method

  • Two predictors, both counted on citations the paper makes, not receives:
    • wi — citations to papers of its own technology cluster.
    • ac — citations to papers of other technology clusters.
  • Both standardised by opportunity, since early papers have fewer papers available to cite:
    • wi divided by the papers published in the same cluster before that year.
    • ac divided by the papers published in all other clusters before that year.
  • Papers with an undefined standardised score, that is no prior papers to cite, are dropped.
  • Overall specification: citations ~ wi + ac + cluster citeability + s(age).
  • Per-cluster specification: citations ~ wi + ac + s(age), fitted separately for each of the 16 clusters.

Appendix E.

This appendix documents the retrieval of the media corpus used in Study 2 and reports the robustness check described in the main manuscript. It follows the order of the main text: search construction, retrieval, the media corpus over time, the cross-sectional attention comparison, and the lag analysis.

Search Construction

For each of the 901 canonical technologies, we generated a title-restricted Media Cloud query and a corresponding Python regular expression using gpt-4.1-mini through the OpenAI API (\(T = 0\); JSON-schema-constrained structured output). Queries consist of wildcard-prefixed terms joined with AND and OR operators, restricted to article titles with the article_title:(...) syntax.

Media Cloud’s title-search operator does not enforce word adjacency or word order, so the query alone returns titles in which the constituent words appear anywhere in any order. We therefore applied the regular expression locally to every retrieved title, requiring the constituent words of the technology term to occur directly next to one another and in the intended order. Titles that did not satisfy the regular expression were discarded as false positives.

Searches ran against the United States – National collection (collection ID 34412234) and covered January 1, 2000, through June 30, 2026. Duplicate stories within a technology were removed. A story matching more than one technology was retained once per technology, mirroring the treatment of multi-technology articles in Study 1. Retained records were stored per technology term and combined into an annual cluster-level series. The full set of search strings and regular expressions is reported in Table 18.

Retrieval

All 901 queries completed. They returned 5,261,184 candidate stories, of which 4,779,807 satisfied the regular expression and were retained, and 4,771,950 fall within the 2008–2026 analysis window. Of the 901 technology terms, 566 returned at least one retained match and 335 returned none.

Rank-Order Agreement Between the Two Corpora

The cross-sectional comparison in the main text contrasts the attention shares themselves. This section reports the same contrast on the ordering of the clusters alone, which asks whether the two corpora agree on which technologies matter most, irrespective of how much attention separates them. Each cluster is ranked within each corpus on its pooled share over the whole period, with rank 1 the cluster holding the largest share, and the two orderings are compared with Spearman’s \(\rho\) and Kendall’s \(\tau\). Ties are averaged. As with the \(G\) test, both corpora are censuses of their retrieval frames rather than random samples, so the \(p\)-values quantify the discrepancy against the null of no rank association rather than sampling error.

The two orderings agree moderately, \(\rho = .634\), \(p = .008\), \(\tau = .494\), \(p = .008\), with a mean absolute rank difference of 3.1 positions across the sixteen clusters. Generative AI carries the largest positive difference, ranking first in the academic corpus against eighth in the media corpus, followed by Gaming & Digital Platforms and Algorithms & Recommendations at six positions each. The largest negative differences are Other and Internet & Broadcasting, which the media corpus ranks third and second against tenth and joint seventh in the academic corpus. Two clusters occupy the same rank in both corpora, Computers & Software at five and Auctions & Pricing at sixteen. Table 19 reports all clusters.

Ranks depend only on the ordering of the pooled counts within each corpus, and both attention bases divide those counts by a denominator that is constant across clusters. The rank order, the rank differences and the two coefficients are therefore identical on the within-technology and the overall basis, and Table 23 reproduces Table 19 for completeness with the shares restated on the overall basis.

The comparisons above treat the academic corpus as one body. Both use the 2008–2026 window and the within-technology basis. Denominators are the journal’s own technology mentions, so a journal is compared with the media benchmark and not with the other journals.

Peak-Year Agreement

The gap table is cross-sectional and says nothing about timing. For each cluster we therefore took the year of maximum attention in the media and the year of maximum attention in each journal, both on a three-year rolling average of the within-technology share, and correlated the two orderings across clusters with Spearman’s \(\rho\) and Kendall’s \(\tau\). Only the ordering enters, so a journal scores high if it reaches its maximum on the technologies the media peaked on early before those the media peaked on late, whatever the size of its attention. Clusters a journal never published on are dropped, which leaves fourteen or fifteen clusters for three of the journals. Table 21 reports the peak years themselves and Table 22 the agreement.

Agreement is weak throughout. Journal of Marketing Research is closest at \(\rho = .450\), followed by Journal of Consumer Research, Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science and Marketing Science between \(.368\) and \(.385\); International Journal of Research in Marketing reaches \(.134\), and Journal of Marketing and Journal of Consumer Psychology sit at zero or below. Median year differences are small and of both signs, from \(-5\) years for Journal of Marketing to \(+1\) year for Journal of Consumer Research and International Journal of Research in Marketing.

Two features of Table 21 limit how far these coefficients can be read. Clusters whose attention was still rising in 2026 peak at the window edge in both series by construction, which raises agreement for every journal alike, and clusters a journal covered with only one or two articles place their peak wherever those articles fall.

Permutation Tests of the Lag Correlations

The cross-correlations reported in the main text are descriptive. Here we attach an exact permutation test to each of them, on both the within-technology basis used in the main text and the overall basis used in the robustness check. A conventional Pearson test does not apply to these series, because annual attention shares are autocorrelated and the observations are therefore not independent.

For each lag, we first aligned the two series at that lag and then shifted the media series circularly against the academic series. A circular shift moves every observation forward by the same offset and wraps the end of the series back to the start, which preserves the internal order and autocorrelation of the media series while breaking its alignment with the academic series. Aligning before shifting keeps the circular shift from being confounded with the lag itself. The \(p\)-value is two-sided,

\[ p = \frac{1 + \#\{\text{shifted correlations with } |r| \geq |r_{\text{obs}}|\}}{1 + \text{number of shifts}}, \]

and we enumerated every available shift rather than sampling from them, which makes the test exact and removes any dependence on a random seed.

Enumeration also fixes the resolution of the test. A series of \(n\) observations admits exactly \(n\) circular shifts, one of which is the identity, so the smallest attainable \(p\)-value is \(1/n\). Aligning at lag \(L\) reduces the overlap to \(19 - |L|\) years, and the number of available shifts equals that overlap. The lower bound therefore depends on the lag: \(1/19 = .053\) at lag 0, rising to \(1/14 = .071\) at lags \(-5\) and \(+5\). No cell in either table can fall below \(.05\), and drawing more permutations than the number of available shifts does not lower this bound, because additional draws only repeat the same arrangements.

The within-technology basis is reported in the main manuscript and Table 24 reports the overall basis, each giving the correlation at every lag with its \(p\)-value in parentheses and the highest correlation in each row set in bold.

On the within-technology basis, the smallest values occur for Generative AI and Search & Advertising, which sit at the per-lag lower bound across a contiguous run of lags. All remaining values lie above \(.05\).

On the overall basis, Generative AI sits at the lower bound at every lag, and Gaming & Digital Platforms and Streaming, Audio & Video sit at or near it across the positive lags. All remaining values lie above \(.05\).
