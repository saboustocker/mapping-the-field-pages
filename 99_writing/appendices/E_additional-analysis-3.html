Appendix E. Supplementary Analyses to Analysis 3

This appendix documents the retrieval of the media corpus used in Study 2 and reports the robustness check described in the main manuscript. It follows the order of the main text: search construction, retrieval, the media corpus over time, the cross-sectional attention comparison, and the lag analysis.

Search Construction

For each of the 901 canonical technologies, we generated a title-restricted Media Cloud query and a corresponding Python regular expression using gpt-4.1-mini through the OpenAI API (\(T = 0\); JSON-schema-constrained structured output). Queries consist of wildcard-prefixed terms joined with AND and OR operators, restricted to article titles with the article_title:(...) syntax.

Media Cloud’s title-search operator does not enforce word adjacency or word order, so the query alone returns titles in which the constituent words appear anywhere in any order. We therefore applied the regular expression locally to every retrieved title, requiring the constituent words of the technology term to occur directly next to one another and in the intended order. Titles that did not satisfy the regular expression were discarded as false positives.

Searches ran against the United States – National collection (collection ID 34412234) and covered January 1, 2000, through June 30, 2026. Duplicate stories within a technology were removed. A story matching more than one technology was retained once per technology, mirroring the treatment of multi-technology articles in Study 1. Retained records were stored per technology term and combined into an annual cluster-level series. The full set of search strings and regular expressions is reported in Table 1.

Retrieval

All 901 queries completed. They returned 5,261,184 candidate stories, of which 4,779,807 satisfied the regular expression and were retained, and 4,771,950 fall within the 2008–2026 analysis window. Of the 901 technology terms, 566 returned at least one retained match and 335 returned none.

Rank-Order Agreement Between the Two Corpora

The cross-sectional comparison in the main text contrasts the attention shares themselves. This section reports the same contrast on the ordering of the clusters alone, which asks whether the two corpora agree on which technologies matter most, irrespective of how much attention separates them. Each cluster is ranked within each corpus on its pooled share over the whole period, with rank 1 the cluster holding the largest share, and the two orderings are compared with Spearman’s \(\rho\) and Kendall’s \(\tau\). Ties are averaged. As with the \(G\) test, both corpora are censuses of their retrieval frames rather than random samples, so the \(p\)-values quantify the discrepancy against the null of no rank association rather than sampling error.

The two orderings agree moderately, \(\rho = .634\), \(p = .008\), \(\tau = .494\), \(p = .008\), with a mean absolute rank difference of 3.1 positions across the sixteen clusters. Generative AI carries the largest positive difference, ranking first in the academic corpus against eighth in the media corpus, followed by Gaming & Digital Platforms and Algorithms & Recommendations at six positions each. The largest negative differences are Other and Internet & Broadcasting, which the media corpus ranks third and second against tenth and joint seventh in the academic corpus. Two clusters occupy the same rank in both corpora, Computers & Software at five and Auctions & Pricing at sixteen. Table 2 reports all clusters.

Ranks depend only on the ordering of the pooled counts within each corpus, and both attention bases divide those counts by a denominator that is constant across clusters. The rank order, the rank differences and the two coefficients are therefore identical on the within-technology and the overall basis, and Table 6 reproduces Table 2 for completeness with the shares restated on the overall basis.

Journal-Level Comparison with the Media Benchmark

The comparisons above treat the academic corpus as one body. This section splits it by outlet and asks the same two questions of each of the seven journals separately: how its allocation of attention across the sixteen clusters departs from the media allocation, and how closely its attention peaks track the media peaks. Both use the 2008–2026 window and the within-technology basis. Denominators are the journal’s own technology mentions, so a journal is compared with the media benchmark and not with the other journals.

Attention Gap by Journal

Table 3 reports one row per cluster, with an Overall column for the pooled corpus and one column per journal. Each cell gives the academic minus media share in percentage points, the media rank minus the academic rank in parentheses, and stars for the Benjamini-Hochberg corrected binomial \(q\)-value computed across the sixteen clusters within that column.

The direction of the field-level gaps is shared by nearly every journal. Generative AI is over-covered in all seven, from \(+3.5\) percentage points in Journal of Marketing Research to \(+17.8\) in Journal of Consumer Psychology, and Internet & Broadcasting is under-covered in all seven, between \(-8.4\) and \(-14.8\) points. Algorithms & Recommendations is over-covered in all seven and Social Media under-covered in all seven. Departures from the shared pattern are few: Search & Advertising is under-covered everywhere except Marketing Science, where it is over-covered by \(3.0\) points, and Computers & Software is under-covered everywhere except Journal of Consumer Research.

Peak-Year Agreement

The gap table is cross-sectional and says nothing about timing. For each cluster we therefore took the year of maximum attention in the media and the year of maximum attention in each journal, both on a three-year rolling average of the within-technology share, and correlated the two orderings across clusters with Spearman’s \(\rho\) and Kendall’s \(\tau\). Only the ordering enters, so a journal scores high if it reaches its maximum on the technologies the media peaked on early before those the media peaked on late, whatever the size of its attention. Clusters a journal never published on are dropped, which leaves fourteen or fifteen clusters for three of the journals. Table 4 reports the peak years themselves and Table 5 the agreement.

Agreement is weak throughout. Journal of Marketing Research is closest at \(\rho = .450\), followed by Journal of Consumer Research, Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science and Marketing Science between \(.368\) and \(.385\); International Journal of Research in Marketing reaches \(.134\), and Journal of Marketing and Journal of Consumer Psychology sit at zero or below. Median year differences are small and of both signs, from \(-5\) years for Journal of Marketing to \(+1\) year for Journal of Consumer Research and International Journal of Research in Marketing.

Two features of Table 4 limit how far these coefficients can be read. Clusters whose attention was still rising in 2026 peak at the window edge in both series by construction, which raises agreement for every journal alike, and clusters a journal covered with only one or two articles place their peak wherever those articles fall.

Robustness Check: Overall Attention Basis

The main manuscript reports attention shares computed within technology-related output only, so that each cluster’s share is measured against the other fifteen clusters. As a robustness check, we recomputed both analyses on an overall basis, in which each cluster’s share is measured against all publications and all newspaper articles of the period, whether technology-related or not. Shares are pooled across the whole period rather than averaged over years, on both bases. The two bases differ only in their denominator; the underlying counts are identical.

Cross-Sectional Comparison

We applied the same procedure as in the main text. The \(G\) test requires the reference proportions to sum to one, so the overall basis adds a residual “not tech-related” category to the sixteen clusters, giving 17 categories and \(n = 7{,}864\) academic publications.

On this basis, the distribution of academic publications departs from the corresponding media proportions, \(G(16) = 2153.96\), \(p < .001\), \(w = .52\), with a total variation distance of \(.11\). Fifteen of the sixteen clusters hold a larger share of academic than of media attention and one holds a smaller share. The ratio of academic to media share ranges from \(1.00\) for Internet & Broadcasting to \(153.66\) for Auctions & Pricing. Fifteen cluster-level tests remain significant after correction; Internet & Broadcasting does not (\(q = 1.00\)).

Permutation Tests of the Lag Correlations

The cross-correlations reported in the main text are descriptive. Here we attach an exact permutation test to each of them, on both the within-technology basis used in the main text and the overall basis used in the robustness check. A conventional Pearson test does not apply to these series, because annual attention shares are autocorrelated and the observations are therefore not independent.

For each lag, we first aligned the two series at that lag and then shifted the media series circularly against the academic series. A circular shift moves every observation forward by the same offset and wraps the end of the series back to the start, which preserves the internal order and autocorrelation of the media series while breaking its alignment with the academic series. Aligning before shifting keeps the circular shift from being confounded with the lag itself. The \(p\)-value is two-sided,

\[ p = \frac{1 + \#\{\text{shifted correlations with } |r| \geq |r_{\text{obs}}|\}}{1 + \text{number of shifts}}, \]

and we enumerated every available shift rather than sampling from them, which makes the test exact and removes any dependence on a random seed.

Enumeration also fixes the resolution of the test. A series of \(n\) observations admits exactly \(n\) circular shifts, one of which is the identity, so the smallest attainable \(p\)-value is \(1/n\). Aligning at lag \(L\) reduces the overlap to \(19 - |L|\) years, and the number of available shifts equals that overlap. The lower bound therefore depends on the lag: \(1/19 = .053\) at lag 0, rising to \(1/14 = .071\) at lags \(-5\) and \(+5\). No cell in either table can fall below \(.05\), and drawing more permutations than the number of available shifts does not lower this bound, because additional draws only repeat the same arrangements.

The within-technology basis is reported in the main manuscript and Table 7 reports the overall basis, each giving the correlation at every lag with its \(p\)-value in parentheses and the highest correlation in each row set in bold.

On the within-technology basis, the smallest values occur for Generative AI and Search & Advertising, which sit at the per-lag lower bound across a contiguous run of lags. All remaining values lie above \(.05\).

On the overall basis, Generative AI sits at the lower bound at every lag, and Gaming & Digital Platforms and Streaming, Audio & Video sit at or near it across the positive lags. All remaining values lie above \(.05\).
